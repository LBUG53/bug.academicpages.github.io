I am a historian of American higher education. I study which programs, relationships, and measures of success university leaders defend when resources run short, and why institutions facing the same pressures defend different priorities. My book manuscript, *Anchors of Identity: The Many Paths to Prestige in the American University*, which Cornell University Press has invited me to submit for peer review, is a comparative history of the graduate schools of education at Harvard, Penn, and Teachers College, Columbia. It shows that academic prestige is only one of several paths to durable institutional success: each school built a lasting identity around the audiences it served, the patrons that sustained it, and the standards by which its faculty judged good work.

I bring a second vantage point to these questions. At Harvard, I am a Strategic Analytics Fellow, developing university-wide standards for how institutional data is defined and used. Before that, I staffed the planning body for Harvard’s first faculty senate. Earlier in my career, as Assistant Director of Research at the Consortium on Financing Higher Education (COFHE), I advised senior leaders at 35 selective colleges and universities on enrollment, financial aid, and student success. I hold a PhD from Harvard and have taught at Brown, Harvard, and the University of Michigan.

## Where this started
I first began thinking about what shapes education in third grade, when I was sent to the principal’s office for asking why our lesson on Columbus left out all of the people his expedition killed. Later, I went to the University of Massachusetts at Amherst to prepare to be a history teacher so I could learn how to teach in the ways my eight-year-old self had needed. But my history and education courses had assumptions embedded in them that made me deeply uncomfortable.  
The more I learned, the more I came to believe that the best way I could contribute to education was by working to make American higher education better – not only through teaching and research, but also by helping institutional leaders make more informed and thoughtful decisions.

## Scholarship
Academic prestige is not the only thing faculty and institutions defend, and the history of who gets heard in those decisions changes how we understand the American university. My article “Beyond Disciplinary Prestige” is forthcoming in *History of Education Quarterly*, and my essay “[A Call for Historically Informed Decisions](https://www.insidehighered.com/opinion/views/2025/06/27/call-historically-informed-decisions-opinion){: target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}” was taken up in Ben Vinson III’s 2026 presidential address to the American Historical Association.

## Teaching
I want students to see the assumptions behind current practice and ask whether they still hold. I’ve taught the history of American higher education to undergraduates, master’s students, and PhD students at Brown, Harvard, and the University of Michigan, starting each course from the questions students bring and building toward a project in which they ask a question of their own.

Approach to Teaching

My teaching is grounded in the belief that everyone working in education needs to understand how assumptions about the past shape present practice. Every policy, funding formula, and governance structure rests on a premise that someone once chose, and most go unexamined by the people who inherit them. Teaching students to see those premises, and to ask whether they still hold, is the skill I most want them to carry out of my courses.

  • Start from students’ questions. I begin each course by asking what issues students most want to explore, and build opportunities to take them up in depth as the course goes on.
  • Plan backward, teach actively. I design lessons with Backward Design principles, informed by my work with Harvard’s Teaching and Learning Lab. Discussion sessions mix whole-class conversation with jigsaws, think-pair-share, and other small-group work. When students are building technical skills, such as data analysis in Python or R, I begin with a follow-along demonstration before moving to practice problems.
  • Read for how an argument is built. I teach students to read for how an argument is constructed as well as for what it concludes, a habit that lets them move between literatures in history, economics, sociology, political science, and human development.
  • Ask a question of your own. At the center of my courses is a project in which students frame, scope, and answer a question of their own, choosing evidence that can actually settle it.
  • Make room for every perspective. Courses on education raise contested questions. I ground discussion in evidence and set the expectation that we engage with ideas and claims without judging the people who share them.
  • Know your students early. I require every student to meet with me during office hours early in the semester, which makes them more likely to participate and to reach out later.

I have taught undergraduate, graduate, and doctoral students, in person, in hybrid settings, and fully online.
